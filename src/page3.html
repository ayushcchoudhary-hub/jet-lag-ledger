349 days out of sync.

I fly a lot, and jet lag wrecks me. So I replayed ten years of my flights through a body clock model, to count the days my body was in the wrong time zone. Then I repeated two published studies on public data, to check that a small shift really matters. The studies are credited below.

What out of sync means

When I land, the wall clock changes at once. My body clock does not. It follows me at about one hour a day.

THE RULE

A day is out of sync when my body clock is 2 hours or more from the wall clock.

BEHINDI flew east. My body thinks it is earlier. It is living in the past.
AHEADI flew west. My body thinks it is later. It is living in the future.
02

Does a small shift matter?

Three published studies say yes. Each one uses a natural experiment: the world runs the test for you. The ideas and the findings belong to the authors. I repeated two of the studies on public data.

A small shift is enough.One lost hour shows in the crash numbers of a whole country.
Direction matters.East is harder than west. So my model recovers more slowly after flying east.
My 2-hour rule is cautious.One hour already shows in crashes. The baseball study counted a team as jet-lagged at 2 hours off. I use the same rule.

Every data source, and what I used it for open ↓

Limits. People recover at different speeds, and light and sleep matter. The model ignores them. A 12-hour shift counts as westward. A daylight saving change at the place I am in makes a 1-hour gap, which is under the 2-hour rule. Flights missing from the export are not counted. The baseball replication adjusts for team, season and ballpark. Its error bars treat every game as independent, so they are too narrow. The paper used a different model. The crash replication compares the week after the change with the two weeks before it and the second and third weeks after it.

Song, Severini, Allada (2017). How jet lag impairs Major League Baseball performance. PNAS 114(6), 1407–1412.
